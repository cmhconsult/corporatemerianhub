Why clear hierarchies make schools faster

Organisational Design • 12 February 2026 • 5 min read

Clarity is not about adding layers. It is about making sure each decision has one owner.

Clarity is not about adding layers. It is about making sure each decision has one owner, each handoff has a named next step, and each team knows where its work begins and ends.

In a practical hierarchy audit, we start by mapping what already exists. Who decides? Who is consulted? Who is informed? Who does the work? When those answers are fuzzy, delays are almost inevitable.

The next step is to ask department leads a simple question: where does work get stuck? The answers usually point to the same places — decisions with two owners, approvals that pass through too many hands, and tasks that everyone assumes someone else is handling.

The fix is rarely a dramatic restructure. It is often a small change: naming one owner, removing a duplicate approval, or writing down a handoff that used to live in someone's head. Small changes, repeated consistently, remove the longest delays.

If your school feels busy but slow, start with clarity. A visible structure gives people permission to act, and gives leaders a calmer way to follow up.

5 workflow leaks costing your institution time

Operations • 28 January 2026 • 6 min read

Most lost time is not dramatic. It hides in the small, repeated steps no one questions.

Most lost time is not dramatic. It hides in approvals, repeated data entry, unclear handoffs and meetings without decisions. The good news is that these leaks are visible once you look for them.

The first leak is duplicate data entry. The same information is typed into a spreadsheet, a system and an email. Every copy is a chance for error — and for wasted time.

The second is the approval loop. A decision passes from person to person, often waiting days at each step. The third is the unclear handoff: work is “sent over” without a named owner or a clear next action.

The fourth is meetings without owners. People leave with good intentions but no named next step. The fifth is tools that do not talk to each other, forcing manual workarounds that everyone has quietly accepted.

For every leak, there is a practical fix that a school can test in a single term. Start with one workflow, follow it end to end, and remove one unnecessary step. Progress is rarely about working harder. It is about working clearer.

From busy to productive: A practical guide for school leaders

Leadership • 14 January 2026 • 5 min read

A calmer operating rhythm starts with fewer priorities and better follow-through.

Busy is not the same as effective. A calmer operating rhythm starts with fewer priorities, clearer ownership and better follow-through.

This guide offers a weekly rhythm for school leaders: a short planning moment, one clear priority per department, a consistent check-in, and a simple close-out. It is deliberately practical.

Start the week by naming what matters most. Not ten priorities — one clear priority per department. When everything is urgent, nothing is.

Then protect the check-in. A short, consistent conversation beats a long, occasional one. Ask what is moving, what is stuck, and who needs help. Close the week by confirming what was finished and what carries forward.

This rhythm is built for leaders who need progress between lessons, admissions, parents and people. Productivity is not about doing more. It is about finishing the right things, together.
